Research Themes

During my Ph.D. and postdoc, my research primarily focused on content-aware networking, traffic engineering in data centers, satellite communication networks, wireless traffic modeling, and algorithmic foundations for sensor networks, summarized below:

  • Video Delivery in Cellular Networks — exploiting video content characteristics to allocate cellular resources by packet importance
  • QAVA: Quota Aware Video Adaptation — adapting video quality to keep users within a monthly data budget
  • Traffic Management in Data Center Networks — semi-centralized architectures for joint rate control, routing, and scheduling
  • Wi-Fi Traffic Modeling in Public Hot-Spots — statistical models of customer arrivals, session durations, and occupancy
  • Fast Data Collection in Sensor Networks — approximation algorithms for scheduling and routing tree design in convergecast
  • Applications of Sensor Networks — structural health monitoring and intelligent parking lot guidance
  • Computational Geometry Methods — coverage, connectivity, and topology control for 3D sensor deployments
  • IP-Routing and Transport in Satellite Networks — joint transport and IP-routing optimization for GEO/LEO satellite constellations

Video Delivery in Cellular Networks

How can cellular networks utilize what's inside a video stream — not just treat it as generic bits — to deliver better quality under constrained network resources, such as limited bandwidth?

As the Internet becomes increasingly popular for sharing multimedia content, we observe a widening gap between how content is produced and distributed vs. how it is transported over the network. Content-providers assume that the communication pipes are dumb, whereas pipe-providers assume that all bits are equal, thus creating a content-pipe divide.

Content-aware networking refers to the methodology of utilizing the content's rate-distortion characteristics (e.g., video encoding, frame type, group-of-pictures or GOP structure) to design adaptive and efficient network protocols.

Tap to view full size ↗

In this work, we develop techniques exploiting such content characteristics to allocate resources in cellular networks based on the importance of video packets. We validate our techniques using real-world measurement data from AT&T and T-Mobile networks, and by conducting testbed experiments on reconfigurable, FPGA-based software defined radios, such as WARP boards.

QAVA: Quota Aware Video Adaptation

How can video streaming applications (e.g., Netflix, YouTube, Hulu) and embedded webpage videos keep users happy without blowing through their monthly data quota?

In recent year, video traffic is surging on both wireline and wireless Internet, just as ISPs like AT&T Mobility and Verizon Wireless began capping unlimited data plans and charging per GB beyond a baseline quota. These two trends are at odds: video consumes more data than ever, while usage-based pricing punishes exactly that.

Given this conflict, is there a way for users to stay within their monthly data plans (quotas) without suffering a noticeable degradation in video quality?

Tap to view full size ↗

In this work, we built an online video adaptation system, QAVA, that manages this tradeoff by leveraging the compressibility of videos and predicting consumer usage behavior throughout a billing cycle. The main modules comprise: (a) Stream Selector, (b) User Profiler, and (c) Video Profiler. We designed online algorithms using dynamic programming and evaluated them using real video request traces. Empirical results suggest that QAVA can provide an effective solution to the dilemma of usage-based pricing of heavy video traffic.

Traffic Management in Data Center Networks

How should large OSPs like Google, Amazon, Microsoft employ traffic engineering to meet the diverse performance requirements of applications hosted in their data centers?

Large online service providers (OSPs) build private backbone networks to interconnect data centers across geographies. These data centers house numerous applications and services that produce multiple classes of traffic with diverse performance objectives. Applications within the same class may also have differences in relative importance to the OSP's core business.

Unlike traditional Internet service providers (ISPs), an OSP can control both the hosts and the routers. Given this flexibility, what is the ideal architecture for wide-area traffic engineering (TE) that an OSP should employ to jointly optimize rate control, backbone routing, and link scheduling to meet the diverse performance objectives of those applications?

Tap to view full size ↗

In this work, motivated by centralized TE approaches like Google's B4 software-defined WAN, we present two semi-centralized architectures that scale by distributing computation across multiple tiers using a few management entities. Using optimization decomposition, we show that both designs are provably optimal, with a tradeoff between convergence rate and message passing. Simulations on realistic backbones (e.g., Abilene topology) show that the 3-tier design is more scalable, but converges slower than the 2-tier design.

Wi-Fi Traffic Modeling in Public Hot-Spots

What does typical Wi-Fi traffic pattern look like in public hotspots like coffee shots, bookstores, hotels, etc. — and can it be modeled well enough to plan network capacity?

The ubiquitous deployment of wireless hot-spots in coffee shops, book stores, malls, hotels, etc. has drawn millions of users onto smaller coverage footprints at considerably higher bandwidth. Prior "workload characterization" studies were mostly limited to laboratory conditions or short-term monitoring at conferences, leaving public, unregulated hot-spots poorly understood.

Tap to view full size ↗

In this work, we developed a common modeling framework to characterize three critical aspects of hotspots that would help model network capacity: (a) customer arrival patterns, (b) connection durations, and (c) simultaneously present users. We combined statistical clustering with Poisson regression to fit a non-stationary Poisson process to the arrival counts. We modeled the heavy-tailed distribution of connection durations via a Phase-Type distribution, and also obtained the distribution of simultaneously present users from an Mt/G/∞ queuing model using a novel regerative argument. Our results were validated using real-world Wi-Fi datasets from hotspots in New York City and San Francisco.

Related publications INFOCOM 2011 Slides

Fast Data Collection in Sensor Networks

How fast can a sensor network gather data from every node without collisions or wasted energy?

Fast and periodic collection of aggregated data is of considerable interest for mission-critical and continuous monitoring applications — target tracking, structural health monitoring, health care — that demand efficient, timely collection of large data volumes despite low sensor-radio bandwidth, wireless contention, and limited power.

Two primary factors that affect the data collection rate and timeliness are: (a) efficiency of the link scheduling protocol, and (b) topology of the routing tree, in terms of its node degrees and radius. The two objectives of maximizing the data collection rate and minimizing the delay are mutually conflicting due to limited network bandwidth, time, and a node's processing power. We ask: Is there a routing topology and a link schedule that can optimize both the objectives with some provable guarantees?

Tap to view full size ↗

In this work, we consider tree-based sensor networks (i.e., where a single sink node collects data from all other nodes) arbitrarily deployed in 2D in which nodes can utilize multiple orthogonal frequency channels to transmit data. We assume a TDMA schedule, and that simultaneous transmissions on orthogonal channels are non-interfering.

We first show that, given a connected network of arbitrarily deployed nodes, a limited number of frequencies, and a routing tree, the joint optimization problem of assigning a frequency to each receiver in the tree, and a time slot to each edge that would maximize the aggregated throughput at the sink is NP-hard (reduction from Vertex Color).

We then design an efficient link schedule that gives a constant factor approximation to the optimal throughput for unit disk graphs, and a logarithmc approximation to general disk graphs. Thereafter, to minimize the maximum delay given a throughput bound, we also design a constant factor bicriteria approximation algorithm to construct a Bounded-Degree Minimum-Radius Spanning Tree. We demonstrate the effectiveness of such algorithms and prove several worst-case performance bounds using graph theoretic tools.

Related publications TON 2011 TMC 2012 MASS 2009 Springer Book Chapter 2011 Slides

Applications of Sensor Networks

Can inexpensive sensors reliably monitor a bridge's health or guide drivers to an open parking spot?

Wireless sensor networks have inspired tremendous research interest across smart homes, marine life monitoring, security-surveillance, healthcare, and automotive applications. We focus on two: structural health monitoring (SHM) and an intelligent parking lot guidance system.

Tap to view full size ↗

For SHM, we design an optimal sensor placement strategy using mode shape analysis and an energy-efficient routing scheme, balancing estimation accuracy against the cost of data collection — motivated by real bridge-collapse catastrophes underscoring the need for reliable monitoring. For parking, we show that a combination of magnetic and ultrasound sensors can accurately detect vehicles, validated through a car-counting experiment in a multi-storied university parking structure lasting over a day.

Related publications SCHM 2012 CTS 2008

Computational Geometry Methods

How do you strategically deploy a sensor network that provides complete coverage of the region of interest and also stays connected — even in three dimensions — using as little transmit power as possible?

Effective network coverage, connectivity, and topology control are fundamental to any sensor network. Coverage-connectivity means deploying an optimal number of sensors at strategic locations so the network stays connected, provides complete coverage of the region of interest, and can route data to a sink. Topology control means adjusting transmit power to reduce interference. Existing 2D techniques don't work in 3D due to the lack of directional node ordering and higher algorithmic complexity.

Tap to view full size ↗

In this work, we use computational geometry tools — Voronoi diagrams, orthographic projections, and spherical Delaunay triangulation — for effective coverage, connectivity, and distributed topology control, including one of the first approaches to opportunistically deploy both static and mobile nodes in a hybrid sensor network.

Related publications Elsevier PMC 2008 John Wiley Book Chapter 2006 LCN 2004 SECON 2007

IP-Routing and Transport in Satellite Networks

How do you route data efficiently across satellites in different orbits (e.g., GEO, LEO) without leaning on ground stations for every hop?

Efficient routing strategies combined with robust transport protocols are paramount in new-generation satellite networks spanning diverse constellations at different orbits. Compared to older bent-pipe satellite systems with no onboard IP-routing, newer generations offer a lot more flexibility — but require investigating cross-layer approaches for heterogeneous GEO + LEO satellite networks.

We propose JTRO-ASN, a joint transport and IP-routing optimization framework for adaptive satellite networks. It establishes end-to-end routes in space using inter-satellite and inter-level SATCOM links, with reduced ground station dependencies, decreased end-to-end latencies, and support for traffic prioritization across multiple available routes.

Tap to view full size ↗

The routing protocol organizes the network into GEO-satellite-centered domains — analogous to Autonomous Systems in the Internet — each tracking inter-satellite links (ISLs) and inter-layer links (ILLs) via time-indexed graphs that predict topology changes from known orbital trajectories, together with a hierarchical addressing scheme for LEO satellites and their footprints. Within each domain, JTRO-ASN solves a multi-class cross-layer optimization problem that jointly computes end-to-end paths and flow rates, modeling per-class utility functions for throughput- and delay-sensitive traffic, and supporting flexible multi-path routing to better exploit the satellite mesh topology than traditional single-path schemes. Routing state is maintained through periodic broadcasts from the GEO satellites and neighbor-discovery "HELLO" messages exchanged between LEO satellites.
